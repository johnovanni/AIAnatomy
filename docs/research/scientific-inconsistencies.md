# Scientific inconsistencies: a running list

Places where the app's models and labs, its lesson texts, or the published record disagree with each other or with
what one would expect. Each one is fixed, disclosed in the app, annotated here or in `docs/decisions.md`, or still
open. Several are worth a closer look in a later stage.

Status key:
- **Fixed**: the lab or text now agrees with the record.
- **Disclosed**: the lab still differs, and the app says so plainly on screen or in the lesson.
- **Annotated**: recorded here and in `docs/decisions.md`, with the reason.
- **Open**: not yet resolved.

Decision numbers (D…) refer to `docs/decisions.md`. Positions are counted from 1, as in the app.

| # | Where | Model/lab behaviour | Research/expectation | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Attention lab, induction experiment (D16) | The first design (8 random tokens, then the same 8 again) is exactly periodic. A layer-1 head with learned positions learned to look a fixed 7 places back, the loss reached its floor by step 200, and no induction head formed (layer-2 scores stayed under 0.1). | Olsson et al. (2022): in-context copying is done by a previous-token head in an earlier layer composing with an induction head in a later layer, which matches by content. | Fixed and annotated | Sequences now have a random prefix of varying length, a segment of 4 to 8 distinct letters, its repeat and random filler, so only content matching works. Result: layer-2 induction scores 0.82 and 0.90, layer-1 previous-token scores 0.40 and 0.23. The shortcut is told as a teaching point in the Attention lesson. |
| 2 | Position lab and lesson (Note N1, D11) | With position vectors removed, the 2-layer Fables model's predictions still depend on order. The "swap two earlier tokens, nothing changes" invariance holds exactly only for one layer. | A natural expectation is that a transformer without positions is order-blind. Haviv et al. (2022) found causal language models without positional encodings still learn positional information, which they attribute to the causal mask. | Disclosed | The lab's note and the Position lesson say that removing positions takes away the direct signal of order, not every trace of it. |
| 3 | Residual lab (logit lens) and lesson | Early rows of "What would the model guess at each stage?" are often poor guesses. | nostalgebraist (2020) applies the final norm and output layer to intermediate layers; the early-layer readings are rough because those parts were trained only on the last layer's output. | Disclosed | The lab's note and the Residual stream lesson say to read early rows as rough; only the last row is the model's own prediction. |
| 4 | Attention lab, induction training (Note N4) | Our runs show a loss drop between about steps 750 and 1,000 (2.52 to 2.18 in the measured run), when the induction scores rise. | Olsson et al. (2022) report a phase change early in training, "visible as a bump in the training loss", in models with more than one layer, of every size they studied. | Disclosed | Our model and data are far smaller and different (letters, 2 layers, attention only). The lab and lesson call the drop "similar to" theirs, not identical, and not guaranteed at a particular step. |
| 5 | Scale lab and lesson | The app shows GPT-2 small as 124 million parameters. | The GPT-2 paper's Table 2 lists 117M (Radford et al., 2019). OpenAI's release repository says the original counts were wrong and names the small model 124M. | Disclosed | Counting from the published shape (vocabulary 50,257, width 768, context 1,024, 12 blocks, feed-forward 3,072, output tied to the token table, as in the released code) gives 124,439,808, which supports 124M. |
| 6 | Tokens lesson | The lesson gives the range of sub-word vocabulary sizes as "tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand". | The build's guidance (spec 5.2, controller ruling) said "tens of thousands to about 200,000". Gemma's published vocabulary is 256k tokens (Gemma Team, 2024), above that bound. | Annotated | Worded to match the sources checked: GPT-2 50,257 and Gemma about 256,000. Claude's current tokenizer is not public, and the lesson says so. |
| 7 | Architecture map lesson (and spec section 1) | The spec describes Claude as "built on a transformer language model". | No Anthropic publication checked for this task (Claude 3 announcement and model card pages, two research posts, Scaling Monosemanticity) was found to state Claude's architecture outright. Templeton et al. (2024) study the "middle layer residual stream" of Claude 3 Sonnet, which is transformer vocabulary. | Annotated | The lessons say only what was checked: Anthropic's research on Claude 3 Sonnet studies its residual stream, and Claude's exact design, size and training data are not public. The Claude 3 model card PDF was too large to fetch and was not read. Anthropic's 2025 circuit-tracing papers on Claude 3.5 Haiku (Ameisen et al., "Circuit Tracing"; Lindsey et al., "On the Biology of a Large Language Model") reportedly discuss its attention and MLP layers: not yet checked; would justify a slightly stronger sentence. Until then no lesson says "Claude is a transformer". |
| 8 | Embeddings lesson | The lesson invites the reader to look for groups (letters, digits, punctuation, spaces) in the trained picture. | In large word-level models, nearby embeddings often belong to related words (textbook). For a tiny character model, how much grouping survives the 2-D projection was not measured. | Open | The lesson hedges ("look for", "rough map") and points to the percentage of spread kept. | 9 | Embeddings lesson (found in UI rework Task 16) | The shipped Fables model's 69 tokens include no digits: the fables text has none, so the trained picture shows only unknown, space or new line, punctuation and letters. | The lesson says "look instead for groups such as letters, digits, punctuation and spaces". | Fixed | A wording error (Task 16 review ruling). The lesson now says "letters, punctuation, and spaces and new lines (digits too, in a model trained on text that has them)". The lab's legend shows only the groups present. |
| 10 | Learning-by-reward lesson (Stage 2A final review, I1) | With the exploration rate at 0, the lab's agent still settles on a 10-step (shortest) route: every number starts at 0 and each move costs 1, so a move once tried scores lower than untried ones and the greedy agent keeps trying new moves (guarded by `TryThis_NeverExploring_FindsATenStepPath_ButLeavesOtherSquaresWrong`). Checked during the fix: with the step cost also at 0, seeds 1 to 10 all still ended on 10-step routes, so the lab does not show a first route being locked in either. | The lesson's paragraph on exploring and its third question said a never-exploring agent "repeats" or "can lock in" the first route that worked, which is the general textbook warning about pure greed. Sutton and Barto (2018, section 2.6) describe how starting values can themselves drive exploring. Strictly, their "optimistic initial values" are higher than the true values; here 0 is above the true value only for squares more than 7 steps from the goal, but it is above what a tried move first learns everywhere. | Fixed | The lesson and the question's explanation now say that with the rate at 0 the agent only takes the move that looks best, that untried moves at 0 at first look better than tried ones, so it still finds a short route while squares away from it keep wrong values. The lesson describes the effect in plain words without calling 0 optimistic; Sutton and Barto (2018), already its source for exploring, cover it in section 2.6. No claim about a step cost of 0 was added, since the lab did not show it. |
| 11 | Memory lab (the Task 9 speed check) | At the same budget (3,000 steps of batch 8, context 64), measured on the same 32 validation windows, the plain Elman RNN reached a validation loss of 1.714 at hidden size 128 (34k parameters) and 1.678 at hidden size 256 (101k), against 1.711 for the shipped 2-layer transformer (113k). | The usual account that attention replaced recurrence (longer-range memory, training in parallel): Vaswani et al. (2017), `vaswani2017` in sources.json, the source lesson 4.8 will cite. A reader might expect the transformer to win clearly on loss. | Disclosed | The gap cannot be called a win either way. The 32 windows hold 32 × 64 = 2,048 predictions with a per-character spread of 1.52 nats (measured on the shipped transformer), so the standard error is about 0.034 (0.042 if each window's mean is one sample, since neighbouring characters are correlated). The largest gap, 0.033, is about one standard error, from a single seed per model. The lesson's contrast must come from the loss-by-position curves and an honest, like-for-like speed comparison (decisions.md, Task 9), not from overall loss. Worth a closer look: several seeds per model, and the shipped RNN (trained in Task 11). Task 15: the shipped RNN at hidden size 256 is the one compared (1.678 against 1.711). Lesson 4.8 and its lab say the two are about the same and why the gap is not meaningful; a checked question (`Memory_TheValidationLossesAreAboutTheSame`) recomputes the standard error of the transformer's per-window losses and checks the gap is under 1.5 of them. Still worth several seeds per model. |
| 12 | Diffusion network (the Task 10 speed check) | With the sampling variance sigma_t^2 = beta_t (as first built), samples landed noticeably further from the shape than with the posterior variance sigma_t^2 = beta-tilde_t = beta_t (1 - AlphaBar(t-1)) / (1 - AlphaBar(t)): after 30,000 steps at hidden size 128 (1,000 samples, sampling seed 2) the mean distance to the shape was 0.067 against 0.056 (Spiral), 0.030 against 0.015 (Circle), 0.053 against 0.038 (Two moons) and 0.047 against 0.032 (Letter S). | Ho et al. (2020), section 3.2, `ho2020`: both choices "had similar results" experimentally; sigma^2 = beta is optimal for x_0 drawn from a unit normal, sigma^2 = beta-tilde for x_0 fixed at one point. | Fixed | Not a contradiction: Ho et al. used T = 1,000 steps with betas up to 0.02; this toy uses T = 100 with betas up to 0.1. Nichol and Dhariwal (2021), section 3.1 and Figure 1 (checked in the paper's text, arXiv 2102.09672), say the two are almost equal except near t = 0, and stay close for more of the process as the number of steps grows; here, at t = 2, beta is about 12 times beta-tilde. A 2-D shape is a thin curve, close to the fixed-x_0 case, and sigma^2 = beta left extra noise near t = 0 that the last steps could not remove. (Measured afterwards: with 10 sampling steps beta-tilde does worse than beta, so the cost of fewer steps is not overstated by beta; decisions.md, Task 10 review.) After the Task 10 review the sampler uses beta-tilde, from each schedule's own AlphaBar (a respaced schedule uses AlphaBar at its own previous step). The shipped and retrain measurements in decisions.md (Task 10 review) are with beta-tilde, with coverage reported alongside so a lower-noise sampler cannot score well by covering less of the shape. |
| 13 | Memory lab, "how far back it remembers" (Task 15) | Measured live with `ContextLoss` on the shared 32 windows, mean loss by characters read (transformer / memory network): 1 character 2.605 / 2.662; 5-8 1.787 / 1.797; 9-16 1.819 / 1.747; 17-32 1.643 / 1.534; 33-64 1.678 / 1.670. On all 292 non-overlapping validation windows the gain from 9-16 to 33-64 characters read is -0.006 ± 0.037 for the transformer and -0.008 ± 0.042 for the memory network (mean ± standard error): both curves fall steeply over the first few characters and then stay level. | The usual account (Vaswani et al. 2017, `vaswani2017`; Bengio, Simard and Frasconi 1994, `bengio1994`): attention links any two positions in one step, while a recurrent network must carry information through every step between, which makes long-range dependencies harder to learn. A reader would expect the transformer to gain more from older characters. | Disclosed | Not a contradiction of the sources: they argue about what can be learned, and these are tiny character-level networks (about 100,000 learned numbers each) trained for 3,000 steps on one book of fables, where the next character depends mostly on the current word. Lesson 4.8 says neither network measurably uses characters more than about 8 back at this size, so the chart does not show the transformer's longer reach; the planned question `Memory_TheTransformerGainsMoreFromLongContext` was renamed `Memory_BothCurvesFallFastThenLevelOff` and worded to the measurement (decisions.md, Task 15). Worth a closer look: a copy test (a random string repeated later in the window), which would show whether either network can use a far-back character when the text demands it, and larger or longer-trained models. |
